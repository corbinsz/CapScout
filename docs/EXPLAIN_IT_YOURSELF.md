# Explain it yourself

One page for an interview. Say it in this order.

**The question.** CapScout estimates the nightly rate a home in Austin, San Diego, or Seattle could advertise on Airbnb. Nights booked and yearly revenue are on the page too, labeled as rough estimates, because the nights model missed a bar set before the test. The letter grade ranks that rough revenue inside the city. The app runs on my computer. It is not a website.

**The data.** I used public Inside Airbnb snapshots, CC BY 4.0: Austin from 16 September 2025, San Diego and Seattle from 16 March 2025. I parsed prices like `$1,234.00`, dropped missing and junk prices outside $25 to $2,000, dropped homes with no review in the last year or blocked every night, dropped duplicate ads, and kept only homes within 60 km of downtown. Austin went from 10,533 rows to 7,720. San Diego went from 12,991 to 8,387. Seattle went from 6,772 to 4,610. I joined census tract income, rent, and home value, checked that almost every home landed in a tract, and then left census out because it did not improve the training error by 2%.

**The test, before the model.** I cut each city into 3 km cells and held out 20% of the cells with seed 17, written down before the model choice. Every home in those cells is held out. I also removed, from training only, any host who has a held-out home and any training home within 1 km. Seeds 19, 29, and 37 repeat that frozen choice. They were not used to pick a feature or a model. Seattle's holdout is 8 cells, so its interval is a resample of those 8 cells.

**The models.** I compared the local median rate for that neighbourhood and room type, linear regression, ridge regression, a random forest, and XGBoost, all on the log of the nightly rate. The rule was: ship the simplest model within 2% of the best error. XGBoost had the lowest average miss, $54.19 a night. Ridge was $55.18. The 2% cutoff was $55.28, so ridge ships.

**The features added after that choice.** The rule, written before the scores, was to add a fixed bundle only if grouped cross-validation on the training cells cut the equal-city average error by at least 2%. The bundle is an inverse-distance index of a fixed public place list, distance to a fixed shoreline list in San Diego and Seattle, and bedrooms times guests. It cleared the bar, at 2.2%. A later check scored the eligible models once on those columns. Linear regression had the lowest equal-city error, $53.63, against $53.67 for XGBoost, $53.95 for ridge, and $54.37 for a random forest. The 2% band goes up to $54.70, so linear regression ships. Counting every held-out home once, R² is 0.571, the median error is 23.0%, and the average error is 37.3% lower than the local median rate (95% interval 34.2% to 42.2%), on 3,648 homes. The average miss is $59.80 against $95.34.

**The catch.** The model underpredicts expensive homes. The top 10% of advertised rates account for about 38% of the absolute error, and the bias on that slice is about −$163. On a later Inside Airbnb snapshot, scored with the previous ridge model and not re-run for linear regression, homes whose listing id and host were not in training are harder: pooled R² 0.328 and a median error of 38.8%, still 26.6% less error than the training-set local median rate. San Diego's R² on the earlier columns moved from 0.182 to 0.650 across the other seeds. Those seeds were not re-run after the added features or the switch to linear regression. The range in the app covered 78.4% of Austin's holdout, 81.0% of San Diego's, and 79.7% of Seattle's. I quote those, and I did not widen the band after seeing them.

**Nights.** The label is reviews in the last year, divided by 0.50, times about 3 nights, capped at 255.5. It is not a count of bookings. Linear regression was within 2% of the best nights model, so that is what ships. It missed a bar I set first: every city needed R² of at least 0.10 and an error at least 10% below the local median nights. Held-out R² is 0.014 in Austin, 0.118 in San Diego, and 0.062 in Seattle. The app calls nights a rough estimate.

**If they ask what I would not claim.** I would not claim profit, a legal short-term rental, or what a guest actually paid. The grade matched the review-based revenue's grade about 34% of the time after the added features. That is a rank of a rough estimate, not a verdict.
